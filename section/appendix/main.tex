\section{Additional Evaluation}
\label{app:eval}

\rev{This appendix reports the counterfactual-\emph{quality} studies that complement the runtime results of Table~\ref{tab:multimodal_comparison}. All studies are reproducible (fixed seeds) and, consistent with the prototype, use synthetic embeddings; the counterfactual-generator comparison uses our own reference reimplementations of each method's core mechanism (not the released libraries, and not UCI data). Extending to large real corpora and released libraries on standard benchmarks (Adult, German-Credit, HELOC) is ongoing.}

\hw{Table~\ref{tab:modality} makes the instance-level semantics concrete for each modality. Each row names (i) the constraint type that defines admissibility, (ii) the form taken by the interpretable edit $\Delta$, and (iii) the stage at which the constraint is cheapest to enforce soundly. For tabular data, column rules and immutability are attribute-level and can be pushed into the index and candidate generation; for temporal data, dynamics and execution validity require checking a candidate sub-trajectory and are enforced during execution-level validation; graph constraints (connectivity, typing, capacity) require structural validation; and text/image admissibility (span rewrites, localized edits) requires semantic validation. The table thus shows feasibility shifting from cheap, index-time checks (tabular) to progressively more expensive validation (temporal, graph, semantic)---precisely the gradient that the coarse-to-fine pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:query-answering} is designed to exploit, applying costly checks only to the small candidate pool.}

\rev{\noindent\textbf{Collective validity (Table~\ref{tab:quality}).} We evaluate the set objectives of Section~\ref{sec:set}---diversity $\Omega_{\text{div}}$, robustness $\Omega_{\text{rob}}$, and fairness $\Omega_{\text{fair}}$---for top-$k$ lowest-cost selection and for objective-specific Set-step selectors ($k{=}5$, pool $m{=}100$, $200$ queries). Top-$k$ is optimal for \emph{none} of the objectives; each objective is best served by its own selector, and diversity and robustness trade off. The pattern holds across text, image, and graph.}

\hw{Reading Table~\ref{tab:quality} column by column confirms that no single selector dominates. On tabular data, the diversity selector raises $\Omega_{\text{div}}$ from $97.12$ (top-$k$) to $117.60$, the robustness selector improves $\Omega_{\text{rob}}$ from $-14.16$ to $-14.09$, and the fairness selector improves $\Omega_{\text{fair}}$ from $-0.179$ to $-0.100$; in every case top-$k$ is beaten in the targeted objective. The effect is far larger on temporal data, where diversity optimization increases $\Omega_{\text{div}}$ more than six-fold ($151.52\!\to\!957.31$). These gains are not free: the same diversity selector raises the mean cost from $14.86$ to $77.09$, and the diversity- and robustness-optimal sets differ, so the objectives genuinely trade off against one another and against cost. Top-$k$ wins only the Cost column. This is direct evidence that collective validity (P4) cannot be reduced to lowest-cost top-$k$ retrieval and requires a first-class, user-specified $\Omega$.}

\rev{\noindent\textbf{Counterfactual capability (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}).} We compare DcCA against reference reimplementations of representative counterfactual generators on validity, feasibility (immutability), in-distribution rate, set diversity, and cost. Data-grounded methods (DcCA, FACE) are in-distribution by construction, but only DcCA also respects immutability (via immutability-matched retrieval); model-centric generators achieve low nominal cost yet are infeasible and off-manifold. DcCA also yields the most diverse valid set. This is a complementary trade-off: model-centric methods minimize nominal cost, while DcCA emphasizes feasibility, realism, and verifiability.}

\hw{Table~\ref{tab:baselines} separates \emph{validity} (does the outcome flip?) from \emph{feasibility} (does the edit respect immutable attributes?) and the \emph{in-distribution} rate (does the result lie on the data manifold?). Almost every method flips the label (validity $\approx100\%$; only GeCo drops to $80.8\%$), so validity alone is uninformative. The decisive columns are feasibility and in-distribution rate: the model-centric generators (DiCE, Wachter, GeCo) are $0\%$ feasible and essentially $0\%$ in-distribution because they freely edit immutable attributes and leave the manifold, while FACE, though $100\%$ in-distribution by construction, respects immutability only $1.9\%$ of the time. DcCA is the only method that is simultaneously $100\%$ in-distribution and substantially feasible ($64.7\%$), and it returns the most diverse valid set ($\Omega_{\text{div}}{=}27.08$). The generators' seemingly lower nominal cost ($1.73$--$1.89$ vs.\ $4.09$) is therefore misleading---it is obtained precisely by making infeasible, off-manifold edits. This is the complementary trade-off: model-centric methods minimize nominal distance, whereas DcCA guarantees feasibility, realism, and verifiability.}

\rev{\noindent\textbf{Approximation and scale (Tables~\ref{tab:ann}--\ref{tab:scale}).} Approximate (IVF-style coarse-quantized) retrieval preserves recall at substantial speedup over exact search, confirming that approximate nearest-neighbor techniques apply to counterfactual retrieval over embeddings. End-to-end latency up to $N{=}5\times10^{5}$ shows set selection is near-constant while retrieval dominates scaling, motivating the constraint-aware index of Section~\ref{sec:query-answering}.}

\hw{Together, Tables~\ref{tab:ann} and~\ref{tab:scale} characterize the efficiency dimension. In Table~\ref{tab:ann}, raising the IVF probe count trades speed for recall: a single probe is $22.7\times$ faster than exact search but recovers only $42.5\%$ of the true neighbors, whereas $\text{nprobe}{=}8$ already attains \emph{perfect} recall@$100$ at a $5.6\times$ speedup, confirming that standard approximate-nearest-neighbor techniques transfer to counterfactual retrieval over embeddings with no measurable quality loss. Table~\ref{tab:scale} then isolates where time goes as the corpus grows to $N{=}5\times10^{5}$: the set-selection step stays essentially constant ($\approx1.5$\,ms) because it operates only on the small candidate pool, while brute-force search grows roughly linearly ($0.10\!\to\!129.09$\,ms) and dominates end-to-end latency at scale. This is exactly the cost that a constraint-aware index (Section~\ref{sec:query-answering}) is designed to remove, and it identifies retrieval---not set optimization---as the scaling bottleneck.}
